A light manufacturing company with 18 employees has finally found the person it had been looking for three months to fill a machinist position. The night before the new hire starts, the owner spends her evening gathering tax forms, writing a welcome note, checking whether IT access was requested, and trying to remember where the badges are kept. The next morning, the new employee waits twenty minutes at the front desk because nobody was sure who was supposed to greet her. Three weeks later, another employee texts on a Friday night to ask for Monday off — the message gets lost between two other conversations, and the team ends up short-staffed without ever seeing it coming.

Nothing dramatic, taken one at a time. But these two scenes repeat themselves, almost identically, across a large share of small businesses: welcoming a new employee and tracking their time off both depend on one person's memory, not on a process. And that improvisation has a price — a measurable one.

## A third of management time swallowed by paperwork

According to Paychex's *2025 Priorities for Business Leaders* survey, conducted in July 2024 among U.S. small-business leaders, **34% of leaders spend more than 10 hours a week on HR administration** — payroll, people management, and tracking hours worked and time off. Even among the smallest companies in the sample (5 to 19 employees), nearly one leader in three still spends 11 hours or more on it every week.

Converted into Canadian dollars (from the original U.S.-dollar figures), the average cost of that time comes to **$4,700 CAD per week**, or close to $244,000 a year — an invisible line item, since it never shows up on a single accounting entry, but one that amounts to a full-time salary spent filling out forms instead of moving the business forward.

## Onboarding: the first real test — and often the first failure

Lost time is only half the problem. The other half, and the more serious one, hits retention directly. A 2025 study by **TalentLMS and BambooHR**, surveying 1,156 U.S. employees hired within the previous twelve months, measured what new hires actually feel during their first weeks. The finding: **52% said the administrative side of their onboarding — paperwork, forms, formalities — took over**, at the expense of real preparation for the job itself. More concerning still, **39% had second thoughts about accepting the job during that onboarding period** — a share that climbs to 49% among Gen Z employees.

In other words: a poorly organized welcome doesn't just cost management time — it puts the hire itself at risk, at the exact moment the company has just spent weeks, sometimes months, recruiting the right person. As we discussed in our article on the [hidden cost of repetitive administrative tasks](/en/blog/cout-cache-taches-administratives/), this kind of invisible work quietly piles up — but in the case of onboarding, it piles up at the worst possible moment, in full view of someone who is still deciding whether to stay.

## Why these two processes stay informal

Onboarding and time-off management share a trait that explains why they so often escape any real structure: they're processes that are **infrequent for any one person**, but **constant at the scale of the business**. An owner might only hire three or four times a year — not often enough, in their mind, to justify building a real system. And a single time-off request takes two minutes to handle — until it has to be cross-checked against ten other requests, a production schedule, and a union agreement.

The result is a process that exists, but exists **nowhere in writing**: it lives in the head of the owner or whoever handles HR, passed from one hire to the next by memory rather than by a checklist. As long as that person is around, things mostly work. The day they're on vacation, out sick, or simply swamped, the process collapses — and it's usually the new hire, or the employee asking for time off, who absorbs the impact.

There's an important difference, though, from processes we've covered before: unlike [double data entry in a CRM](/en/blog/en-finir-double-saisie-crm/), where the information is structured (a name, an amount, a date), a large part of onboarding touches on things that are fundamentally human — the first impression, the introduction to the team, the feeling of being expected. Automating that part blindly would backfire. The right target isn't onboarding itself, but everything around it that has no reason to be improvised.

## What can — and should — be structured

The method that works looks like the one described in our article on [workflow engineering](/en/blog/ingenierie-flux-travail/): separate what's predictable and repetitive from what requires human judgment, then automate only the first category.

**For onboarding**, the predictable part is actually most of the administrative work:

- Automatic delivery of forms (tax, banking, internal policies) as soon as the offer is signed, with e-signature
- Scheduled creation of computer access and email accounts, triggered by the start date instead of forgotten until the morning of
- An automatic reminder to whoever is responsible for the welcome, the day before, with a clear checklist: who greets them, at what time, with what equipment ready
- A first-week itinerary sent ahead of time, so the new hire knows what to expect instead of guessing

None of this replaces the handshake, the introduction to the team, or the first real project handed over. It simply frees up the time and attention needed to do that part well — the part that, precisely, can't be automated.

**For time-off requests**, the structure is even easier to put in place: a single form (not three different channels — text, email, and a hallway conversation) that automatically updates a shared calendar, notifies the right people according to clear rules, and keeps a searchable history. This isn't a months-long project: for most small businesses, it's an afternoon of setup that eliminates years of lost messages.

## What automation shouldn't do

Two warnings are worth stating clearly, so the fix doesn't recreate the problem it's meant to solve.

- **Don't replace human welcome with automated messages.** A system that only sends generic emails on day one, with no real colleague picking up from there, makes the exact problem behind that 52% statistic worse: more forms, no more feeling of being welcome.
- **Don't over-engineer what doesn't need it.** A company with three or four employees probably doesn't need a full HR platform — a simple shared checklist and a time-off form can be enough for years. The right question is never "which tool should we buy," but "which step keeps getting missed right now."

## Where to start

There's no need to rebuild the entire HR function at once. The next time a hire is coming up, simply write down every step you currently do from memory — and ask which one has already been missed at least once in the past two years. That's where to start. Structure that step, measure the time and peace of mind it buys back, then move to the next one.
